The North Wind
and the Sun

A fable credited to Aesop, about two ways of getting what you want. Scroll to hear it told. At the end, the weather is yours.

The North Wind and the Sun were arguing about which of them was the stronger. Each had a long list of proofs, and neither had ever persuaded the other of anything.

Then a traveller came along the road below, wrapped in a heavy red cloak. They agreed on a test. Whichever of them could get the cloak off his back would be called the stronger. The Wind went first.

The North Wind drew a breath that flattened the grass for a mile around, and blew.

The poplars bowed. Dust stood up off the road. But the harder the Wind blew, the tighter the traveller pulled the cloak around him, and the lower he bent his head.

At last the Wind was out of breath, and the cloak had not shifted an inch. “Your turn,” said the Wind, and did not say it kindly.

The Sun was in no hurry. First came a little light, and the traveller straightened his back. Then came warmth, and he pushed up his hat and undid the clasp at his neck.

By the top of the next hill the cloak was off and slung over one shoulder, and he was whistling. The Wind had to admit that the Sun was the stronger. It took the rest of the afternoon to say so.

Persuasion is better than force.

The moral, as George Fyler Townsend gave it in 1867

Your turn

Here he comes again, cloak and all. You have a wind and a sun. Get the cloak off him.

He’s walking easy, cloak on.

Where this fable has been

It is short enough to memorise and old enough that nobody owns it. Every hand it passed through changed it a little.

  1. Around 600 bce

    Told aloud in Greece and credited, as most good fables were, to Aesop. In Greek the two are Boreas and Helios. Scholars file the story as number 46 in the Perry Index, the standard catalogue of the tradition.

  2. Around 400 ce

    Avianus puts it into Latin verse as De Vento et Sole, the fourth fable in a collection that schoolrooms went on copying for centuries.

  3. 1668

    Jean de La Fontaine retells it in French verse as Phébus et Borée, the third fable of his sixth book.

  4. 1867

    George Fyler Townsend’s English translation closes on the five words most people remember: persuasion is better than force.

  5. 1949

    By now the International Phonetic Association prints the fable as its specimen text. A linguist describing the sounds of a language records a speaker reading this story, which is how it came to exist in well over a hundred languages.

  6. 1998

    Kim Dae-jung, newly president of South Korea, names his policy of engagement with the North after the fable: the Sunshine Policy.

One more reading

The fable is usually read as a win for gentleness. It is also a story about who gets to choose the test.

Had the wager been to make the traveller put a cloak on, the North Wind would have won before the Sun was properly up. You can check that above: take the cloak off him, then bring back the wind.
